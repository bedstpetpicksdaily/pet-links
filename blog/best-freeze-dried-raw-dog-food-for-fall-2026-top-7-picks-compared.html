Best Freeze-Dried Raw Dog Food for Fall 2026: Top 7 Picks Compared Zeal Pet Food

✓Last updated: September 03, 2026
⏱12 min read
By Best Pet Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Freeze-dried raw has quietly become the default upgrade for owners who want raw nutrition without a freezer full of thawing patties. But the category has gotten crowded, and the marketing language across brands is nearly identical: "95% meat, organs and bone," "minimally processed," "no fillers." Once you strip that away, the real differences come down to three things — where the protein actually comes from, how much of the bag is meat versus produce and supplements, and what you pay per pound of finished food after rehydration.

This comparison puts Zeal Pet Food, a New Zealand–sourced brand best known for its grass-fed and wild-caught proteins, up against six competitors most US shoppers will run into: Stella & Chewy's, Primal Pet Foods, Instinct Raw Longevity, Vital Essentials, Open Farm and Northwest Naturals. We looked at sourcing transparency, ingredient panels, pellet or patty format, availability, and cost per feeding for a 30 lb dog.

Check the ingredient list carefully to ensure the freeze-dried meat is the primary ingredient and not filler, and verify that the product matches your pet's dietary needs (grain-free, limited ingredient, etc.) since formulations vary significantly across brands.

Editor's Tip

Fair warning: there is no single winner here. A brand that's excellent as a full-time diet for a picky 12 lb terrier is often a poor value as a topper for an 80 lb shepherd. Below, each pick is matched to the situation where it genuinely makes the most sense.

1. Zeal Pet Food — Best for New Zealand sourcing and single-origin proteins

Zeal's pitch is geographic rather than technological: proteins come from New Zealand's grass-fed beef and lamb, free-range chicken, and wild-caught green-lipped mussel and hoki (a cold-water whitefish). That last one matters — green-lipped mussel is a naturally occurring source of omega-3s and glucosamine-type compounds, and Zeal includes it across much of the range rather than reaching for a synthetic joint additive. New Zealand's biosecurity rules also mean no ractopamine and no routine hormone use in livestock, which is a genuine sourcing distinction rather than a marketing line. The freeze-dried recipes are grain-free and lean heavily on the meat/organ/bone ratio, with modest produce inclusion. Practically, the pieces rehydrate quickly and hold together well as a topper, and Zeal also runs a strong air-dried line if you'd rather scoop-and-serve with no water at all. The catch: US distribution is thinner than Stella & Chewy's or Primal, so you're more likely to order direct than grab a bag at the local pet store. 2. Stella & Chewy's Freeze-Dried Raw Dinner Patties — Best availability and easiest to find on sale

Stella & Chewy's is the volume leader, and that's its main advantage. You can find the Dinner Patties in nearly every independent pet store and most major online retailers, typically around $40–$50 for a 25 oz bag of chicken or beef, with frequent promotions that bring the effective cost down meaningfully. The formulas are 95% meat, organs and ground bone, with certified organic fruits and vegetables and a probiotic coating. Where it trails Zeal is sourcing specificity — S&C uses a mix of North American and imported ingredients and is less granular about farm origin. The patty format is also chunkier than Zeal's pieces, so you'll want to crumble and soak for 3–5 minutes for smaller dogs. For most owners, this is the practical default: good quality, wide selection of proteins, and you'll never struggle to restock.

3. Primal Pet Foods Freeze-Dried Nuggets — Best for organic produce and protein variety

Primal's nuggets are small, roughly dice-sized, which makes portioning far easier than patties. The ingredient decks include certified organic produce and a notably broad protein lineup — pork, venison, rabbit, quail, duck, sardine — which is a real asset for dogs on elimination diets or with chicken and beef sensitivities. Pricing typically lands around $38–$55 for a 14 oz bag, making Primal one of the more expensive options per ounce. Compared to Zeal, Primal packs in more vegetable and supplement content, which some owners like and others read as unnecessary filler in a raw diet. Primal is also the most likely of this group to appear in a store's freezer aisle alongside its frozen raw line, so you can cross-shop formats.

4. Instinct Raw Longevity Freeze-Dried Bites — Best for age-specific formulation

Instinct (owned by Nature's Variety) took a different angle with Raw Longevity: separate formulas for puppies, adults and seniors, plus a post-biotic blend. The bites are small and easy to use as a kibble topper — which is largely how Instinct positions them, since the brand also sells the popular Raw Boost kibble. Expect roughly $30–$45 for a 9.5–17.5 oz bag. Relative to Zeal, Instinct offers more fortification and life-stage targeting but less of a clean, single-origin ingredient story. If you're mixing raw into an existing kibble routine rather than feeding raw exclusively, Instinct's ecosystem is the most seamless.

5. Vital Essentials Freeze-Dried Mini Patties — Best minimalist ingredient panel

Vital Essentials is the purist of the group: most recipes are single-protein, with no added fruits, vegetables, or synthetic vitamin premix — just meat, organs and bone. For dogs with genuine multi-ingredient sensitivities, that short list is a real clinical advantage, and the brand does its own sourcing and processing in Wisconsin. Pricing runs about $35–$50 for a 14 oz bag. The trade-off is that many recipes aren't formulated to meet AAFCO complete-and-balanced profiles the way Zeal's and Stella & Chewy's dinners are, so read the bag carefully if this is meant to be the whole diet rather than a topper.

6. Open Farm Freeze-Dried Raw — Best traceability and ethical sourcing

Open Farm's headline feature is a lot code lookup that traces every ingredient in your specific bag back to its farm or fishery, plus Certified Humane and Global Animal Partnership-audited animal welfare standards and ocean-caught fish certified by Ocean Wise. It is the most transparent brand here on paper — arguably more so than Zeal, though Zeal's New Zealand pastoral system delivers a similar practical outcome with less documentation. Open Farm typically sits around $40–$50 for a 13.5 oz bag, putting it at the premium end. Recipes include modest amounts of produce like pumpkin and coconut oil.

7. Northwest Naturals Freeze-Dried Nuggets — Best budget entry into freeze-dried raw

Northwest Naturals is the value pick. Sourcing is Pacific Northwest–based, formulas are simple, and pricing often lands under $35 for a 12 oz bag — noticeably cheaper per ounce than Primal or Open Farm. The nuggets are small and rehydrate fast. Quality control history is reasonable but the brand has had a recall in its past, as have several competitors here, so it's worth checking current FDA notices. If your goal is to add some raw to a kibble bowl without doubling your food budget, this is where to start.

How the seven compare at a glance

On pure sourcing pedigree, Zeal and Open Farm lead. On availability and promotional pricing, Stella & Chewy's is untouchable. On protein variety for allergy dogs, Primal wins. On the shortest possible ingredient list, Vital Essentials. On cost per ounce, Northwest Naturals. And on life-stage tailoring for a kibble-plus-raw routine, Instinct. Zeal's specific edge is the combination of New Zealand grass-fed proteins with green-lipped mussel built into the recipe rather than bolted on — a formulation choice that's hard to find elsewhere at any price.

Our Verdict

If sourcing quality is your deciding factor and you're comfortable ordering direct, Zeal Pet Food is the pick — the New Zealand grass-fed proteins and whole-food green-lipped mussel give it a formulation profile that's genuinely hard to replicate, and the air-dried option is a useful fallback for travel or camping. But be honest about your situation. If you need to walk into a store and grab a bag on a Sunday night, Stella & Chewy's is the smarter buy and goes on sale constantly. If your dog has diagnosed protein allergies, Primal's rabbit and quail formulas solve a problem Zeal can't. If you're feeding a large dog and the budget is the constraint, Northwest Naturals delivers most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost per ounce. And if you want farm-level traceability documented on the bag, Open Farm beats everyone. Match the brand to your actual constraint rather than chasing the highest-spec option — a mid-tier freeze-dried raw you can afford to feed consistently beats a premium one you ration.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is freeze-dried raw as nutritious as frozen raw?

Close, but not identical. Freeze-drying removes water at low temperature, preserving most nutrients and enzymes far better than cooking or extrusion. Some heat-sensitive vitamins degrade slightly, which is why brands like Zeal, Stella & Chewy's and Instinct add a vitamin and mineral premix to hit AAFCO complete-and-balanced profiles. Frozen raw retains marginally more, but freeze-dried wins massively on convenience, shelf stability and pathogen risk management.

Do I need to rehydrate freeze-dried raw dog food?

It's strongly recommended. Adding warm water for 3–5 minutes improves digestibility, boosts hydration and releases aroma that helps with picky eaters. Patty-format foods like Stella & Chewy's benefit most; smaller nuggets from Primal, Northwest Naturals or Zeal rehydrate faster. If you want a genuinely no-water option, Zeal's air-dried range is designed to be served dry straight from the bag.

How much does it cost to feed a 30 lb dog freeze-dried raw full time?

Expect roughly $6–$10 per day across most premium brands in this comparison, since a 30 lb dog eats around 6–8 oz of freeze-dried food daily. Northwest Naturals sits at the lower end, Primal and Open Farm at the higher end. Many owners split the difference by feeding freeze-dried as a 25% topper over a quality kibble, which brings daily cost down to $2–$3 while still adding fresh protein to the bowl.
