Freeze-Dried vs. Dehydrated Pet Treats: Which Premium Snack Is Worth Your Money? PetTreatery

Photo: Sebastian Luna / Pexels

⏱12 min read
By Best Pet Picks Daily • September 03, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Walk down the treat aisle of any premium pet store and you'll hit the same fork in the road: freeze-dried or dehydrated (air-dried)? Both promise minimally processed, single-ingredient nutrition. Both cost three to five times what a bag of supermarket biscuits does. And both are marketed with almost identical language — "raw goodness," "nutrients locked in," "nothing artificial." The differences are real, but they're not the ones the packaging shouts about.

The short version: freeze-drying removes moisture at sub-zero temperatures under vacuum, preserving close to raw nutrient levels and producing a light, crumbly, almost styrofoam-like texture that most cats prefer. Dehydration and air-drying use gentle heat over many hours, producing a chewier, denser, jerky-like treat that lasts longer in a dog's mouth and typically costs 30–50% less per ounce. Neither is universally "better" — they're better at different jobs.

Check the freeze product's temperature range and thaw time to ensure it matches your pet's needs—some products are designed for short-term cooling relief while others are meant for extended freezer storage, so verify which type suits whether you need a quick chew toy or a long-lasting frozen treat.

PetTreatery: Broad range, curated selection, best for multi-pet households

PetTreatery positions itself as a curated premium treat retailer rather than a single manufacturer, which is its biggest structural difference from the brands below. Instead of committing to one process, it stocks freeze-dried meat and fish morsels, dehydrated jerky strips, natural chews and cat-specific snacks side by side — so a household with a picky cat and a strong-jawed dog can consolidate one order instead of three. In practice that matters for shipping economics: buying a 3 oz bag of freeze-dried cat treats from one brand and a bag of dehydrated dog jerky from another often means two lots of shipping fees that erase the savings on the treats themselves. The trade-off is transparency. Because the range spans multiple suppliers, sourcing detail varies by product — some listings specify country of origin and single-protein formulation clearly, others are vaguer than Ziwi's or Vital Essentials' packaging. If you're feeding an allergy dog on a strict elimination protocol, read each individual product page rather than assuming a house standard applies across the catalogue. For everyday high-value training treats and rotation variety, though, the breadth is genuinely useful, and pack prices generally undercut the boutique single-brand competition.

Stella & Chewy's Carnivore Crunch: The freeze-dried benchmark

Stella & Chewy's is the brand most vets and trainers name first when you say "freeze-dried treats," and Carnivore Crunch is the flagship. A 3.25 oz bag typically runs $11–$14, which works out to roughly $3.50–$4.30 per ounce — expensive, but you're paying for 98% meat, organs and bone with added probiotics, and for a manufacturing operation with genuine scale and a solid recall record. The treats are bite-sized cubes that crumble easily, which makes them excellent for breaking into pea-sized pieces during recall training. The downside is exactly that crumbliness: the bottom of the bag is often dust, and a determined Labrador swallows a cube without registering it as a reward. Cats, on the other hand, tend to go feral for the chicken and duck varieties. If you want one product that reliably works as a high-value training currency for a small-to-medium dog or a fussy cat, this is the safest default purchase in the category.

Vital Essentials: Single-ingredient purity at a premium price

Vital Essentials takes the strictest line on ingredients — many of its treats are literally one thing: freeze-dried beef liver, chicken hearts, or whole minnows, with no binders, no added vitamins, no probiotics, nothing. That makes them the go-to for elimination diets and for owners who want to read a label in under two seconds. The catch is cost per ounce, which is often the highest in this comparison: small bags of the freeze-dried minnows or 0.9–1 oz specialty items can push well past $10, and the beef nibblets in larger 5.5 oz sizes commonly sit around $22–$28. The whole minnows are also polarising — cats and many dogs adore them, humans find the smell hard to live with. Buy Vital Essentials when ingredient control is a medical necessity or when you need an intensely motivating reward for a dog that ignores everything else. Don't buy it as your everyday, hand-out-twenty-a-day training treat unless budget is genuinely no object.

Ziwi Peak Air-Dried Treats: The dehydrated heavyweight

Ziwi Peak is the strongest argument for the air-dried side of the debate. Its New Zealand-sourced venison, lamb and green-lipped mussel treats are slowly air-dried rather than freeze-dried, producing a dense, slightly leathery texture that a dog has to actually work at. That's the practical advantage: an air-dried chew occupies a bored dog for thirty seconds rather than half a second, and the treats don't disintegrate in a training pouch or a coat pocket. Ziwi's sourcing transparency is also best-in-class — the brand names its supply region and farming standards openly. It is not cheap; small 3 oz pouches frequently land in the $10–$16 range, and the green-lipped mussel line costs more still. Ziwi also runs into the classic dehydrated-treat limitation: gentle heat does reduce some heat-sensitive nutrients relative to freeze-drying, though for a treat that makes up 10% of daily calories, that's a nutritional rounding error rather than a real concern.

Head-to-head: what actually differs

On price per ounce, PetTreatery's dehydrated and jerky-style lines generally come in below Ziwi and Vital Essentials, with Stella & Chewy's sitting mid-pack. On ingredient transparency, Vital Essentials and Ziwi Peak lead clearly, Stella & Chewy's is close behind, and PetTreatery varies by product. On texture and use case, freeze-dried (Stella & Chewy's, Vital Essentials) wins for cats and rapid-fire training; air-dried and dehydrated (Ziwi, much of PetTreatery's dog range) wins for chew time and pocket durability. On convenience, a multi-brand retailer like PetTreatery wins on one basket, one shipping charge and easier variety rotation.

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here — there's a right answer per pet and per job. If you have a cat or you're doing high-frequency reward-based training with a small-to-medium dog, Stella & Chewy's Carnivore Crunch is the most reliable buy in the category and worth the roughly $4 per ounce. If your dog is on a veterinary elimination diet or you refuse to accept any ingredient you can't pronounce, Vital Essentials is the purist's choice, and you'll pay the highest per-ounce price for that discipline. If you want a treat that actually lasts more than a second and you value verifiable sourcing above all, Ziwi Peak's air-dried range is the strongest dehydrated option on the market. PetTreatery makes the most sense for the very common real-world scenario the boutique brands ignore: a household with more than one animal, different texture preferences, and a monthly treat budget that doesn't stretch to three separate premium orders. Buy it for range, value and convenience — and check the individual product listing for sourcing detail before you buy it for a pet with a diagnosed allergy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are freeze-dried treats actually more nutritious than dehydrated ones?

Marginally, yes. Freeze-drying removes moisture at sub-zero temperatures, so heat-sensitive nutrients and enzymes survive better than under the low heat used in dehydration and air-drying. But treats should be under 10% of daily calories, so the difference is nutritionally trivial for most pets. Texture, price and how motivating your animal finds them matter far more in practice.

Why are freeze-dried treats so much more expensive per ounce?

Two reasons. Freeze-drying is a slow, energy-intensive vacuum process that costs more per batch than heated air-drying. And because the water is removed, an ounce of freeze-dried meat started life as roughly four to five ounces of raw meat — you're paying for the original weight, not the finished weight. That's why a 3 oz bag can cost $12 or more across every brand in this comparison.

Can I give my cat dog treats from the same brand?

For single-ingredient freeze-dried meat, usually yes — plain freeze-dried chicken or beef liver is fine for both, though liver should be limited due to vitamin A content. Avoid anything with added plant ingredients, garlic, onion or supplements formulated for canine requirements. Size matters too: crumble dog-sized pieces down before offering them to a cat, and check with your vet if your pet has any existing health condition.
